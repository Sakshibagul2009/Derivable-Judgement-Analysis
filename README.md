# 📚 Part A – Theoretical Foundation

## 1. Inferential Statistics

Inferential statistics is a branch of statistics used to draw conclusions about a population based on sample data.

It includes methods such as hypothesis testing, confidence intervals, and statistical estimation.

In this project, inferential statistics helps evaluate differences and relationships among health-related variables.

## 2. Hypothesis Testing

Hypothesis testing is a statistical method used to determine whether sufficient evidence exists to support a claim about a population.

Its main components are:

- **Null Hypothesis (H₀):** Assumes that there is no significant difference or association.
- **Alternative Hypothesis (H₁):** Assumes that a significant difference or association exists.
- **Significance Level (α):** The threshold used to make a statistical decision.
- **Test Statistic:** A value calculated from the sample data.
- **p-value:** Measures how compatible the observed result is with the null hypothesis.
- **Critical Value:** A boundary used to determine the rejection region.

The project uses a significance level of **0.05** for the statistical tests.

## 3. Confidence Interval

A confidence interval provides a range of plausible values for a population parameter based on sample data.

The formula used is:

**Confidence Interval = Mean ± Critical Value × Standard Error**

A 95% confidence interval is calculated for age, weight, BMI, glucose level, and blood pressure.

## 4. Critical Value

A critical value is a threshold used to determine whether a test statistic falls within the rejection region of a statistical test.

It depends on the significance level and the relevant probability distribution.

## 5. p-value

The p-value indicates how extreme the observed result would be if the null hypothesis were true.

At a significance level of 0.05:

- If p-value < 0.05, reject H₀.
- If p-value ≥ 0.05, fail to reject H₀.

A statistically significant result does not automatically establish causation or practical importance.

## 6. Type I and Type II Errors

| Error | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Type I Error | Rejecting a true null hypothesis |
| Type II Error | Failing to reject a false null hypothesis |

These errors represent possible mistakes when making decisions based on sample data.

## 7. Z-test

A Z-test is used to test hypotheses about population means or proportions under suitable assumptions. It is commonly applied when the population variance is known or when an appropriate large-sample approximation is justified.

## 8. t-test

A t-test is used to compare means under suitable assumptions, particularly when the population standard deviation is unknown.

This project uses an independent two-sample t-test to compare glucose levels between diabetic and non-diabetic individuals.

## 9. Chi-square Test

The Chi-square test is used to investigate whether two categorical variables are associated.

In this project, it examines the association between smoking status and diabetes.

## 10. ANOVA

Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) is used to compare means across three or more groups.

In this project, one-way ANOVA is applied to compare diabetes indicator means across age groups.

Because the diabetes indicator is binary, interpreting these group means as diabetes proportions is possible. However, the usual ANOVA assumptions and the suitability of the method should also be considered.

## 11. Covariance

Covariance measures the direction in which two numerical variables vary together.

- Positive covariance indicates that the variables tend to move in the same direction.
- Negative covariance indicates that the variables tend to move in opposite directions.

## 12. Correlation

Correlation measures the strength and direction of the relationship between two numerical variables.

The Pearson correlation coefficient ranges from -1 to +1.

| Correlation Value | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Close to +1 | Strong positive linear relationship |
| Close to -1 | Strong negative linear relationship |
| Close to 0 | Weak or no linear relationship |

# 📊 Part B – Statistical Analysis and Testing

## 1. Formulation of Hypotheses

Three main hypotheses are formulated for statistical testing.

### Hypothesis 1: Smoking Status and Diabetes

**Null Hypothesis (H₀):**

Smoking status has no significant association with diabetes.

**Alternative Hypothesis (H₁):**

Smoking status has a significant association with diabetes.

**Statistical Test:** Chi-square test of independence.

### Hypothesis 2: Glucose Level and Diabetes

**Null Hypothesis (H₀):**

The mean glucose level is the same for diabetic and non-diabetic individuals.

**Alternative Hypothesis (H₁):**

The mean glucose level differs between diabetic and non-diabetic individuals.

**Statistical Test:** Independent two-sample t-test using Welch's method.

### Hypothesis 3: Age Group and Diabetes

**Null Hypothesis (H₀):**

The mean diabetes indicator is the same across all age groups.

**Alternative Hypothesis (H₁):**

At least one age group has a different mean diabetes indicator.

**Statistical Test:** One-way ANOVA.

## 3. Find the Critical Value and p-value

Critical values and p-values are used to evaluate the statistical significance of the tests.

The significance level is set to:

**α = 0.05**

### Decision Rule

- Reject H₀ when the p-value is less than 0.05.
- Fail to reject H₀ when the p-value is greater than or equal to 0.05.

### Interpretation

According to the recorded results:

- **t-test:** The p-value is less than 0.05, so H₀ is rejected.
- **Chi-square test:** The p-value is greater than 0.05, so H₀ is not rejected.
- **ANOVA:** The p-value is less than 0.05, so H₀ is rejected.

The final decision is based on the statistical evidence provided by each test.

---

## 4. Independent Two-Sample t-test

### Objective

To determine whether mean glucose levels differ significantly between diabetic and non-diabetic individuals.

### Hypotheses

**H₀:** There is no significant difference in mean glucose levels between diabetic and non-diabetic individuals.

**H₁:** There is a significant difference in mean glucose levels between diabetic and non-diabetic individuals.

### Formula

\[
t = \frac{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}
{\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}
\]

Where:

- \(\bar{x}_1\) and \(\bar{x}_2\) are the group means.
- \(s_1\) and \(s_2\) are the group standard deviations.
- \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) are the group sample sizes.

### Results

| Measure | Result |
|---|---:|
| Mean Glucose Level – Diabetic | 131.4482 |
| Mean Glucose Level – Non-diabetic | 99.7737 |
| t-statistic | 37.8708 |
| p-value | 3.2644 × 10⁻¹¹⁵ |
| Significance Level | 0.05 |
| Decision | Reject H₀ |

### Interpretation

The mean glucose level is higher in the diabetic group than in the non-diabetic group in this dataset.

The p-value is much smaller than 0.05. Therefore, the null hypothesis is rejected.

**Conclusion:** There is a statistically significant difference in mean glucose levels between diabetic and non-diabetic individuals in the analyzed dataset.

---

## 5. Chi-square Test: Smoking Status and Diabetes

### Objective

To determine whether smoking status is significantly associated with diabetes.

### Hypotheses

**H₀:** Smoking status has no significant association with diabetes.

**H₁:** Smoking status has a significant association with diabetes.

### Methodology

1. Create a contingency table using smoking status and diabetes.
2. Calculate the Chi-square test statistic.
3. Calculate the degrees of freedom.
4. Determine the critical value.
5. Compare the p-value with the significance level.

### Results

| Measure | Result |
|---|---:|
| Chi-square Statistic | 2.091 |
| Degrees of Freedom | 2 |
| Critical Value | 5.991 |
| p-value | 0.3515 |
| Significance Level | 0.05 |
| Decision | Fail to Reject H₀ |

### Interpretation

The p-value is greater than 0.05. Therefore, the null hypothesis is not rejected.

**Conclusion:** The analysis does not provide sufficient statistical evidence of an association between smoking status and diabetes in this dataset.

This result does not prove that no association exists; it indicates that the observed data do not establish a statistically significant association at the selected significance level.

---

## 6. One-Way ANOVA: Age Group and Diabetes

### Objective

To examine whether diabetes rates differ significantly across age groups.

### Hypotheses

**H₀:** The mean diabetes indicator is the same across all age groups.

**H₁:** At least one age group has a different mean diabetes indicator.

### Formula

\[
F = \frac{MS_{\text{Between}}}{MS_{\text{Within}}}
\]

Where:

- \(MS_{\text{Between}}\) represents the mean square between groups.
- \(MS_{\text{Within}}\) represents the mean square within groups.

### Results

| Measure | Result |
|---|---:|
| F-statistic | 25.821 |
| p-value | 2.19 × 10⁻²⁰ |
| Degrees of Freedom – Between Groups | 4 |
| Degrees of Freedom – Within Groups | 995 |
| Critical F-value | 2.381 |
| Decision | Reject H₀ |

### Interpretation

The p-value is much smaller than 0.05, and the F-statistic exceeds the recorded critical F-value.

Therefore, the null hypothesis is rejected.

**Conclusion:** The analysis indicates a statistically significant difference in the mean diabetes indicator across age groups.

Because ANOVA does not identify which particular groups differ, additional post-hoc comparisons would be needed to determine the specific differences between age groups.

---

## 7. Calculate Covariance and Correlation: Age and BMI

### Objective

To examine the relationship between age and BMI using covariance and Pearson correlation.

### Hypotheses

**H₀:** There is no significant linear correlation between age and BMI.

**H₁:** There is a significant linear correlation between age and BMI.

### Results

| Measure | Result |
|---|---:|
| Covariance | 4.231 |
| Pearson Correlation (r) | 0.0424 |
| p-value | 0.1804 |
| Significance Level | 0.05 |
| Decision | Fail to Reject H₀ |

### Interpretation

The covariance is positive, indicating that age and BMI tend to vary in the same direction in this dataset.

However, the Pearson correlation coefficient is close to zero, indicating a very weak positive linear relationship.

The p-value is greater than 0.05. Therefore, the null hypothesis is not rejected.

**Conclusion:** There is no statistically significant linear correlation between age and BMI in the analyzed dataset at the 5% significance level.
